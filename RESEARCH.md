# Research Foundation

RedForce AI is grounded in ongoing doctoral research on the organisational determinants of successful AI-driven cybersecurity adoption in enterprise environments. The research and the platform advance together. Research findings inform platform design. Platform deployment generates empirical data that validates and extends the research.

This document describes the research foundation, the literature gaps it addresses, and how the platform operationalises the research findings.

---

## The Research Question

The doctoral research investigates a question that practitioners and academic researchers have identified as critical but inadequately addressed in the existing literature:

**What organisational determinants influence the successful adaptation of AI-driven cybersecurity systems in enterprise environments?**

This question matters because the cybersecurity industry has spent significant resources building AI security tools, while organisational outcomes from AI adoption remain inconsistent. Some organisations integrate AI security capabilities effectively. Many do not, despite access to equivalent tooling. The difference is not technical. It is organisational.

The research identifies the structural, governance, capability, and adaptation factors that distinguish successful enterprise AI cybersecurity adoption from unsuccessful adoption. The findings inform both academic understanding of organisational technology adoption and the practical design of platforms intended to support that adoption.

---

## The Literature Gap

The reviewed literature consistently identifies organisational gaps that existing AI security tools do not address:

**Readiness assessment gap.** Organisations lack structured methods to assess their readiness for adopting AI-driven cybersecurity. Adoption decisions are made on instinct or vendor pressure rather than systematic capability evaluation.

**Governance maturity gap.** AI governance in enterprises is frequently immature or fragmented. Policies are inconsistent across business units. Accountability for AI security outcomes is unclear. Oversight mechanisms are weak or absent.

**Integration gap.** Organisations struggle to integrate AI security tools and findings into existing cybersecurity operations, change management processes, and business workflows. Tools deploy as islands rather than capabilities.

**Capability translation gap.** Existing AI security tools produce vulnerability findings. They rarely translate findings into organisational capability improvements. The journey from "we found these issues" to "we improved how we handle this class of issue" is left to the customer.

**Maturity measurement gap.** Organisations lack standardised approaches to measuring AI cybersecurity capability maturity over time. Improvement is anecdotal. Strategic decisions about security investment lack quantitative basis.

**Human-AI collaboration gap.** The operational integration of human security analysts with AI security tools is poorly developed. Trust calibration, explainability requirements, and decision-support workflows are afterthoughts in most tool designs.

**Organisational learning gap.** Repeated security assessments rarely produce organisational learning. Each assessment is treated as a discrete event rather than a data point in continuous capability development.

**Executive evidence gap.** Technical security findings rarely translate into governance evidence that executives, boards, and regulators can act on. Security operations and strategic decision-making remain disconnected.

**Adaptation gap.** AI security testing is generally disconnected from organisational adaptation, change management, and digital transformation initiatives. Security operates in parallel to organisational change rather than as part of it.

**Continuous monitoring gap.** Few platforms support continuous monitoring of organisational progress toward secure AI adoption. Annual audits produce snapshots, not trajectories.

These are not theoretical concerns. They appear consistently across the practitioner literature, the academic management literature on technology adoption, and the empirical case studies of enterprise AI deployments that have failed to deliver expected security outcomes.

## How the Research Informs the Platform

The research is not separate from the platform. It directly informs platform design in several ways.

**The organisational capability layer exists because the research identified that technical findings alone do not produce security outcomes.** The platform includes readiness assessment, governance maturity scoring, and capability development tracking because the research established these as the determinants of successful adoption.

**The compliance framework integration exists because the research identified that disconnect between technical evidence and executive decision-making is a primary failure mode.** The platform maps findings to recognised frameworks so the same data serves engineering, compliance, governance, and executive audiences.

**The continuous assessment model exists because the research identified point-in-time validation as insufficient for sustained AI security.** The platform supports scheduled execution, differential reporting, and longitudinal capability tracking so organisations can demonstrate progress over time.

**The reasoning layer exists because the research identified that integration of findings across silos is a critical missing capability.** Conventional tools produce findings in silos; the research showed that organisations cannot translate silo'd findings into action. The platform's converged reasoning integrates findings the way the research showed human analysts need them integrated.

**The modular execution model exists because the research identified that maturity varies across organisations.** A single delivery model fits no one well. The platform supports incremental adoption where organisations engage with capabilities at their current maturity level and expand as maturity grows.

## How the Platform Informs the Research

The research benefits from the platform in ways academic research without an operational platform cannot achieve.

**Empirical validation.** Platform deployment generates data that validates or challenges research hypotheses. Organisational capability scores, governance maturity assessments, and adaptation indicators across diverse customer organisations provide an empirical base that pure interview-based research cannot match.

**Pattern identification.** Cross-organisational analysis of capability development trajectories identifies patterns that individual case studies miss. Organisations that successfully build AI security capability follow recognisable patterns; the platform makes those patterns visible.

**Intervention research.** Beyond observing organisations, the platform enables limited intervention research where capability development recommendations from the research are tested against organisational outcomes. This moves the research from descriptive to potentially prescriptive.

**Longitudinal data.** Continuous assessment across customer organisations produces longitudinal data that supports research on capability development over multi-year timescales — data that is otherwise difficult to acquire.

## Research Publication Plan

Research outputs from the doctoral work and the platform deployment publish through standard academic channels.

**Initial publication** focuses on the converged static-dynamic-documentation analysis model and its empirical validation against industry benchmark targets. This paper establishes the technical contribution and provides the empirical foundation for subsequent organisational research.

**Subsequent publications** address the zero-egress AI security model for regulated industries, the organisational determinants framework developed through the doctoral research, and the longitudinal capability development patterns observed through platform deployment.

Submission targets include OWASP AppSec Research, BlackHat Academy, ACM CCS workshop tracks, and journal venues in information systems and management of information technology. The research is positioned to contribute to multiple academic communities — technical security research, information systems research, and management research on organisational technology adoption.

## Engaging With the Research

The research foundation is open for substantive academic and practitioner exchange.

Researchers working on adjacent questions — organisational determinants of cybersecurity capability development, enterprise AI adoption frameworks, governance maturity models, or human-AI collaboration in security operations — are invited to engage. Cross-pollination strengthens the work.

Practitioners with experience of enterprise AI security adoption, including failed adoptions and partial adoptions, are equally welcome to engage. Practitioner evidence is essential to grounding the research in operational reality rather than theoretical abstraction.

The research is not held proprietary. Findings publish through standard academic channels. The platform that operationalises the research is proprietary, but the research foundation is shared with the broader community.

## A Note on the Combination

It is uncommon for an enterprise security platform to be grounded in active doctoral research, and it is uncommon for doctoral research to have a working operational platform supporting it. The combination is intentional.

Research without operational validation produces frameworks that practitioners cannot use. Operational platforms without research grounding produce tools that work but cannot explain why. RedForce AI is built on the conviction that the most useful contribution to enterprise AI security is one that does both — operates effectively in practice and contributes to the underlying academic understanding of what makes effective adoption possible.

The combination is asymmetric in its advantages. The research strengthens the platform. The platform strengthens the research. The outcome of both, over time, is intended to be a clearer practitioner and academic understanding of how enterprises can successfully adopt AI-driven cybersecurity as a sustained organisational capability rather than a periodic compliance exercise.
